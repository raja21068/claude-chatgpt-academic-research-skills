MID-LLM: Enhancing Medical Image Diagnostics
With LLMs in a Blockchain AI Framework
Rajesh Kumar
, Yunbo Rao
, Jay Kumar
, Member, IEEE, Cobbinah B. Mawuli, Member, IEEE,
Waqar Ali

Abstract—The rapid growth of medical imaging data presents
signiﬁcant challenges in diagnostic accuracy, data privacy, and
computational efﬁciency. Traditional centralized AI models strug-
gle with scalability and pose risks to patient conﬁdentiality due to
data aggregation. Moreover, heterogeneous medical data across
institutions complicates the development of robust diagnostic
tools. To address these issues, we propose MID-large language
model (LLM), a novel framework that integrates LLMs with
a blockchain-based federated learning (FL) system for medical
image analysis. It also ensures the security and privacy of sensi-
tive medical data across decentralized networks. MID-LLM uses
veriﬁcation mechanisms to ensure the global model’s integrity. It
also employs aggregation techniques to reduce bias and improve
training efﬁciency. Experiments on the BraTS 2020 dataset show
that MID-LLM outperforms traditional FL, achieving higher
Dice scores with improved computational efﬁciency. These results
highlight MID-LLM’s potential to enhance diagnostic accuracy
while offering a scalable, secure solution for AI in healthcare.
Index Terms—Blockchain, decentralized AI, interplanetary ﬁle
system (IPFS), large language model (LLM), medical image
data.

I. INTRODUCTION
L
ARGE Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated
exceptional capabilities across various domains, particu-
larly in natural language processing, by enabling machines to
understand and generate human-like text [1]. Their success has
inspired their application in many ﬁelds, including healthcare,
where accurate interpretation of complex data is crucial for
patient care. Integrating LLMs for healthcare care (speciﬁcally
in the context of medical imaging) can potentially improve the
interpretation of complex visual data, leading to more accurate
diagnostics and patient outcomes.
Medical imaging plays a vital role in diagnostics, providing
detailed visual information that aids in the detection and treat-
ment of diseases. The growing volume of medical image data
challenges centralized systems that struggle to process data
efﬁciently while considering data privacy and security [2], [3].
In particular, the centralized nature of these systems raises
concerns about single points of failure and vulnerability
to data breaches [4]. Therefore, handling a large volume
of medical data raises privacy, accuracy, and scalability
issues.
Privacy: Medical data is inherently sensitive and conﬁden-
tial, making privacy a critical concern. Centralized systems
often aggregate large volumes of such data, thereby increas-
ing the risk of security breaches and unauthorized access.
These risks can lead to violations of privacy regulations
and erosion of patient trust. Federated learning (FL) has
emerged as a promising paradigm to address these chal-
lenges, offering a decentralized approach that ensures data
privacy and reduces communication costs by allowing multiple
devices to collaboratively train a machine learning model
without sharing raw data [5]. Unlike traditional distributed
learning methods, FL enables sharing of locally trained
model parameters instead of raw data. This approach sig-
niﬁcantly mitigates privacy risks and aligns with regulatory
requirements [6].
While FL has been extensively studied in deep learning
contexts, its application within reinforcement learning (RL)
remains relatively underexplored. Recent frameworks, such as
federated deep RL (FDRL), mark early efforts to integrate FL
principles into RL tasks, with the goal of enhancing data and
model privacy [7]. However, these frameworks face signiﬁcant
limitations, including vulnerability to single-point failures
caused by centralized aggregators, which can be exploited
through adversarial techniques, such as model poisoning and
bias attacks [4].

Robustness and Accuracy: A major challenge in developing
reliable diagnostic tools is the diversity of medical data
resulting from heterogeneous sources. Variations in equipment,
clinical protocols, and patient demographics can introduce
biases that negatively impact the accuracy of AI models.
LLM-based models is among the popular approach in the
past few years. LLMs are capable to interpret the medical
images by merging visual attributes with relevant clinical data.
LLMs generate textual explanations of anomalies (e.g., tumor
morphology) and can correlate them with medical literature.
For example, an LLM can identify a tumor in an MRI scan and
contextualize its size/location with potential diagnoses (e.g.,
glioblastoma versus metastasis), bridging the gap between raw
image data and clinical decision-making [8], [9].
Scalability: The increasing amount of medical imaging data
calls for scalable and efﬁcient solutions. Centralized systems
often face challenges with computing power and storage,
making it hard to manage large datasets effectively.
LLMs provide helpful context and text-based explanations
that support the visual analysis done by specialized vision
models [10]. This combination helps healthcare providers
make more accurate diagnoses while protecting patient pri-
vacy and data integrity [11]. This approach enhances AI
capabilities while enabling collaboration across healthcare
institutions, allowing them to share insights without com-
promising data integrity. Such collaboration leads to more
robust models that improve AI performance across diverse
clinical settings [12], [13]. Recent advancements in FL show
promise for improving privacy and scalability in healthcare.
Kumar et al. [2] demonstrated blockchain-based FL for brain
tumor segmentation, ensuring patient conﬁdentiality. Similarly,
BAFFLE [4] and federated batch normalization (FedBN) [14]
have strengthened defenses against adversarial attacks and
data heterogeneity. However, these frameworks often lack
integration with advanced AI models for interpretative tasks.
The application of LLMs in medical imaging is gaining
attention of researchers due to their ability to bridge visual
data with clinical context. Wang et al. [15] proposed a
multimodal federated ensemble framework using vision LLMs
for one-shot learning, achieving state-of-the-art performance.
However, this approach does not address local LLM train-
ing costs or blockchain-based security. Other studies, such
as [8], [10], integrated LLMs with vision transformers for
generating descriptive reports but rely on centralized data
aggregation, posing privacy risks. To fully realize the potential
of this integrated approach, it is essential to address key
research questions that will guide its effective implementation
and adoption.
1) RQ1: How does the integration of LLMs improve the
performance of FL for medical image analysis?
2) RQ2: What mechanisms can be implemented to enhance
security and ensure data integrity in a decentralized
medical image analysis framework?
3) RQ3: How can blockchain and interplanetary ﬁle system
(IPFS) technologies be utilized to address privacy and
scalability challenges in FL for medical imaging?

FL offers a powerful solution. Combining FL, blockchain,

II. BACKGROUND
A. Large Language Models
LLMs are transformer-based deep learning architectures
trained on extensive text corpora to perform tasks like text
generation, translation, and contextual understanding. Examples
include BERT and GPT-3 [10], [16], [17]. BERT is a data
representation model that excels in understanding context,
while GPT-3 is a generative model capable of producing
coherent text. In the context of medical imaging, LLMs can be
integrated to enhance the interpretation and analysis of complex
visual data. Recent studies have demonstrated the effectiveness
of LLMs in medical image processing, such as generating
radiology reports and enhancing diagnostic accuracy through
textual descriptions [18], [19]. Although medical images are
inherently visual, the combination of image processing models
with LLMs enables the extraction of meaningful textual
descriptions from images, bridging the gap between visual
data and clinical language. This approach is supported by
advancements in vision-language models like CLIP [20], which
have been adapted for medical imaging tasks [21], [22], [23],
[24], as well as multimodal LLMs that interpret 3-D medical
images [25], [26], [27]. LLMs can process visual embeddings
through soft prompts, integrating image data with clinical text to
enhance diagnostic interpretation, addressing the limitations of
vision models in providing interpretable outputs. This process
is illustrated in Fig. 2, where medical images are encoded
and processed alongside instructions by an LLM to generate
a structured report.

C. Blockchain and Smart Contracts
Blockchain technology provides a decentralized, secure
ledger system that ensures transparency and immutability for
transaction records across a peer-to-peer network. Each node
maintains a copy of the blockchain, updated through consensus
mechanisms like Proof of Work or Proof of Stake [29].
Smart contracts, a key innovation of blockchain technology,
are self-executing contracts with terms written directly into
code. These contracts, running on platforms, such as Ethereum,
execute automatically when predeﬁned conditions are met [30].
Written in languages like Solidity, smart contracts eliminate the
need for intermediaries by enforcing and verifying contractual
agreements automatically. This enhances security and trust
in FL systems by managing and verifying model updates,
ensuring only valid contributions are integrated into the global
model [28]. Fig. 4 illustrates this architecture, where deep
learning models are stored via IPFS, validated through smart
contracts, and coordinated on a distributed blockchain ledger.
III. PROPOSED METHODOLOGY
This section describes the MID-LLM framework (Fig. 1
and Fig. 5), which integrates blockchain, smart contracts, and
advanced machine learning to enable collaboration among hos-
pitals (clients) and research centers (workers). The framework
ensures data privacy and security while enhancing medical
image diagnostics. Below, we outline its key components and
processes.
1) Data Collection and Local Training: Hospitals collect
medical images (e.g., X-rays, MRIs, CT scans) and train
local models using a three-step pipeline: a) an image
encoder extracts features, converting pixel data into
representations; b) a Q-Former processes query tokens
for tasks like tumor detection; and 3) a projection layer
generates soft prompts, enabling the LLM to analyze
images.
2) Parameter Sharing: After local training, hospitals locally
store model parameters and upload them to IPFS as
tamper-proof identiﬁers (hashes). IPFS enables decen-
tralized, secure, and efﬁcient data access. These hashes
are then shared with the blockchain network via the
GlobalSC smart contract, with all transactions recorded on
the blockchain to ensure transparency and immutability.
3) Blockchain Interaction: The GlobalSC smart contract
facilitates the sharing of hashes, enabling research centers
to aggregate the model parameters. This process ensures
transparency and immutability, as all transactions are
permanently and securely recorded on the blockchain.
Workers Research Centers:
1) Parameter Aggregation: Research centers retrieve hashes
from IPFS to access locally trained model parameters
from hospitals. These parameters are aggregated to form
a global model, leveraging FL to incorporate diverse
datasets without exchanging raw data. This improves
diagnostic model robustness across heterogeneous clin-
ical environments.
2) Model Update: The aggregated global model param-
eters are updated and reuploaded to IPFS, with new
hashes distributed across the blockchain network via the
GlobalSC smart contract. This ensures all participants
can securely and transparently access the latest model in
real time, maintaining synchronization while preserving
data integrity.
Blockchain Integration (Smart Contracts):
1) registerSC: Registers hospitals and research centers by
issuing private and public keys to enable secure com-
munication.
2) globalSC: Manages parameter sharing via IPFS and
maintains the global model’s baseline parameters.
3) rewardSC: Distributes rewards based on participants’
contributions to incentivize honest participation.
IPFS Integration (Secure Storage and Retrieval): The
IPFS enables secure, decentralized storage and retrieval of
machine learning model parameters via cryptographic content
addressing, ensuring data integrity through immutable hash
identiﬁers. This framework allows healthcare institutions and
research collaborators to efﬁciently share and access trained
parameters using unique cryptographic hashes, avoiding the
scalability bottlenecks and centralization risks of conventional
cloud storage. By preserving privacy-critical data locality,
IPFS supports federated medical imaging workﬂows while
enhancing system-wide data availability.
A. Bootstrapping
The bootstrapping stage consists of the following two key
steps.
1) Registration of Participants: Participants enroll via
the registerSC smart contract, depositing crypto-assets
into the rewardSC to commit to the process. Each
participant receives a key pair for secure blockchain
communication.
2) Global Model Initialization:
A client initializes the
global model with random parameters and sets hyperpa-
rameters, such as learning rate and iteration count. These
parameters are uploaded to IPFS, with the hash-link
stored in the globalSC smart contract for accessibility.

V. RESULTS
A. Prototype Implementation
Our MID-LLM framework is built using a blend of
advanced technologies and programming libraries tailored
for robust performance in decentralized environments. We
use Python 3 for the primary development of Client and
Worker nodes, leveraging the PyTorch library for constructing
and training deep neural network models tailored to medical
image processing. The OpenAI Gym library is employed
to simulate environments, facilitating varied and extensive
learning scenarios. The code is available at https://github.com/
raja21068/MID-\gls{LLM}/.
For efﬁcient communication between Client and Worker
nodes, HTTP REST API calls are orchestrated via an
API Gateway Server implemented in Node.js. This server
employs an event-driven, nonblocking I/O model that enhances
throughput and scalability in server-side applications. Rapid
data exchange is facilitated by a Redis cache, optimizing the
retrieval and storage of parameter updates.
Blockchain functionality is supported by Hyperledger Fabric
v1.4, which provides a robust and secure platform for manag-
ing decentralized transactions. Smart contracts crucial to the
operation of MID-LLM are crafted using the Go programming
language. The framework employs LLaMA-13B [36], a 13-
billion-parameter decoder-only transformer model, ﬁne-tuned
for medical image analysis. The model is initialized with
weights from a pretrained medical vision-language model,
RadFM [37], which integrates contrastive learning on paired
radiology images and reports. Fine-tuning is performed using
LoRA [31] on the BraTS 2020 dataset, with rank r = 8
and scaling factor α = 16, reducing trainable parameters by
90%. The LLM processes soft prompts generated from frozen
ResNet-50 image embeddings (extracted from MRI sequences)
via cross-attention layers. Training utilizes mixed-precision
(FP16) on NVIDIA A100 GPUs (40GB VRAM), with each
client requiring 18GB VRAM per training epoch.
To manage the ﬂow of data efﬁciently between the API
Gateway Server and the blockchain, we incorporate a Message
Broker designed on Node.js, utilizing Redis for message
queuing services.
