Abstract 
The performance of microbial fuel cells (MFCs) is strongly affected by membrane composition and operating 
conditions. Therefore, identifying the best values of MXene loading, initial chemical oxygen demand (COD), 
and anode pH is essential for improving power generation and wastewater treatment performance. This study 
proposes an artificial neural network (ANN)-based framework for modelling and multi-objective optimization 
of a sulfonated polyether ether ketone (SPEEK)/MXene nanocomposite proton exchange membrane (PEM) in 
a two-chamber MFC. Three input variables were considered: MXene loading (0-7 wt%), initial COD of the 
anode medium (1000-3000 mg L⁻¹), and anode electrolyte pH (5.5-8.5). Four output responses were evaluated: 
power density (PD), COD removal efficiency (CODr), coulombic efficiency (CE), and internal resistance (RI). 
A feed-forward multilayer perceptron trained using the Levenberg-Marquardt backpropagation algorithm 
achieved an overall coefficient of determination (R²) of 0.96, confirming its ability to simulate the nonlinear 
interaction among the investigated variables. The trained ANN response surface was then used for multi-
objective optimization. The best operating condition was identified at MXene = 4.2 wt%, COD = 2300 mg L⁻¹, 
and pH = 7.1, with predicted values of PD = 612 mW m⁻², CODr = 66%, CE = 16.2%, and RI = 63 Ω. 
Experimental validation in triplicate produced PD = 617.6 mW m⁻², CODr = 67.4%, CE = 17.2%, and RI = 
64.5 Ω. The overall prediction accuracy was 97.1%, with a mean absolute error of 2.4% across the four 
outputs. SEM analysis confirmed that 4.2 wt% MXene suppressed biofouling and EPS deposition, whereas 
excessive loading caused nanosheet agglomeration and surface defects. The proposed ANN-based framework 
provides a reliable data-driven approach for SPEEK/MXene MFC design and can be extended to other 
nanocomposite membrane systems for wastewater treatment and bioelectricity recovery. 
1. Introduction 
The global demand for decentralized wastewater treatment technologies capable of recovering energy from 
organic wastes has intensified interest in bioelectrochemical systems. Among these systems, the two-chamber 
microbial fuel cell (MFC), separated by a proton exchange membrane (PEM), is considered a promising 
configuration because it can treat wastewater and generate electricity simultaneously. 
In an MFC, exoelectrogenic microorganisms oxidize biodegradable substrates in the anode chamber and 
release electrons and protons through their metabolic activity. The electrons are transferred through an external 
circuit to the cathode, while the protons migrate through the PEM and participate in oxygen reduction at the 
cathode. Accordingly, the measured current is directly linked to substrate degradation and can be used as an 
indicator of both bioelectricity generation and wastewater treatment efficiency [1,2]. Although many attempts 
have been made to improve and commercialize MFCs, practical large-scale application is still restricted by low 
volumetric power output, ohmic and mass-transfer losses, membrane cost, and biofouling at the membrane-
electrolyte interface. These limitations indicate that membrane composition and operating conditions should be 
optimized together, because their effects on microbial activity, proton transport, and electrochemical 
performance are strongly coupled. 
The PEM is the central functional element of a two-chamber MFC. It conducts protons from the anode to the 
cathode, separates the anolyte and catholyte, limits substrate crossover and oxygen back-diffusion, and 
maintains stable electrochemical operation. Nafion 117 has widely been used as a benchmark PEM because of 
its high proton conductivity and chemical stability. However, its high cost, oxygen permeability, biofouling

tendency under high organic loading, and non-selective cation transport limit its practical application in 
wastewater-based MFCs [3,4]. Therefore, the development of low-cost, mechanically stable, and biofouling-
resistant PEM alternatives remains necessary. 
Sulfonated polyether ether ketone (SPEEK) has emerged as one of the most suitable alternative PEM materials 
for MFC applications. SPEEK is obtained by sulfonating polyether ether ketone (PEEK), and its degree of 
sulfonation can be adjusted by controlling reaction temperature and time. Increasing the sulfonation degree 
generally enhances proton conductivity through the formation of hydrophilic sulfonic acid domains. However, 
excessive sulfonation can increase water uptake and swelling, weaken the membrane mechanically, and reduce 
proton selectivity [5]. Thus, bare SPEEK alone cannot fully overcome the trade-off between conductivity, 
dimensional stability, and selectivity. Incorporating inorganic nanofillers into the SPEEK matrix is an effective 
approach for improving proton transport while preserving membrane integrity and reducing crossover. 
Among the available nanofillers, Ti₃C₂Tₓ MXene is particularly attractive because of its two-dimensional 
layered structure, intrinsic hydrophilicity, high electrical conductivity, and abundant surface functional groups, 
including -OH, -F, and =O. When incorporated into the SPEEK matrix, MXene nanosheets can provide 
hydrogen-bonded proton transport pathways, reinforce the polymer network, and reduce bacterial adhesion 
through hydrophilic surface repulsion [6,7]. These characteristics make SPEEK/MXene membranes promising 
for improving MFC performance. Nevertheless, the MXene content must be carefully optimized, because 
excessive loading can lead to nanosheet agglomeration, pore blockage, increased tortuosity, and reduced ion 
transport [8,9]. 
Optimizing this coupled membrane-bioelectrochemical system by conventional one-factor-at-a-time 
experimentation is inadequate, since this approach neglects the interaction effects among membrane 
composition, substrate concentration, and electrolyte pH. Response surface methodology can provide useful 
regression-based information, but polynomial models may not fully describe the nonlinear response surfaces 
produced by microbial kinetics, electrochemical transport, and polymer morphology. Artificial neural networks 
(ANNs) can overcome this limitation by learning the mapping between inputs and outputs directly from 
experimental data without imposing a predefined response-surface form. ANN models have shown strong 
predictive capability for MFC power density and COD removal [10,11]. However, the simultaneous use of 
membrane composition and operating conditions in a single multi-output optimization framework has not been 
sufficiently explored for SPEEK/MXene MFC systems. 
This work addresses this gap by developing an integrated ANN-based modelling and multi-objective 
optimization framework for a SPEEK/MXene nanocomposite PEM MFC. The main contributions are as 
follows. First, MXene loading is treated as a decision variable together with initial COD and anode pH using a 
dataset of 50 experimental observations. Second, the ANN simultaneously predicts four coupled responses, 
namely PD, CODr, CE, and RI, enabling multi-criteria evaluation of MFC performance. Third, the ANN-
predicted optimum is experimentally validated in triplicate to confirm the predictive reliability of the model. 
The generated response surfaces also provide insight into the interaction between membrane composition, 
substrate concentration, pH, and bioelectrochemical performance. 
2. Materials and Methods 
2.1. Membrane Fabrication 
SPEEK/MXene membranes containing 0, 1, 3, 5, and 7 wt% Ti₃C₂Tₓ MXene were fabricated by wet phase 
inversion. Ti₃C₂Tₓ MXene (purity 99 wt%, Suzhou Beike Nanotechnology Co., Ltd., China; flake lateral size 
1-5 μm; interlayer spacing 0.98 nm) was first dispersed in anhydrous N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF; purity 
≥99.8%, Sigma Aldrich) by probe ultrasonication at 500 W and 20 kHz for 5 min. The ultrasonication step was 
performed at 25 °C using ice-bath cooling to avoid solvent heating. The resulting dispersion was visually 
inspected for colloidal stability, and no visible sedimentation was observed over 24 h. SPEEK (degree of 
sulfonation 65%, molecular weight 40,000 g mol⁻¹, Sigma Aldrich) was dissolved separately in DMF at 45 °C 
under magnetic stirring at 300 rpm for 4 h. The MXene dispersion was then added to the SPEEK solution and 
stirred at 45 °C for 5 h. The dope solution was degassed under vacuum at 45 °C for 30 min, cast on a clean 
glass substrate using a doctor blade with a nominal gap of 250 μm, exposed to ambient air for 15 s, and

measured at five positions using a digital micrometer, and the average thickness was within 100-120 μm [16]. 
2.2. MFC Configuration and Media 
A two-chamber MFC was fabricated using transparent plexiglass chambers with a working volume of 300 mL 
for each chamber. The anode and cathode chambers were separated by the SPEEK/MXene nanocomposite 
membrane and sealed with silicone gaskets to prevent leakage. The effective membrane area exposed to the 
electrolyte was 20 cm². Untreated carbon paper (4 cm × 5 cm, Toray TGP-H-060) was used as the anode, while 
the cathode was prepared from carbon paper coated with Pt at 0.35 mg cm⁻² using a brush-painting method and 
5% Nafion ionomer as binder. The anode electrolyte contained glucose as the main carbon source and yeast 
extract as a nitrogen and cofactor source in phosphate buffer saline (PBS; 50 mM; adjusted pH). Initial COD 
was adjusted by changing the glucose concentration within 1000-3000 mg L⁻¹ and was confirmed by UV-Vis 
spectrophotometry before each experiment. Anode pH was adjusted using 0.1 M HCl or 0.1 M NaOH. Before 
inoculation, the anode chamber was sparged with 99.99% nitrogen gas for 15 min and maintained under 
anaerobic conditions. Domestic wastewater sludge collected from a municipal wastewater treatment plant in 
Riyadh was used as the inoculum at 10% v/v. The cathode electrolyte (50 mM PBS, pH 7.0) was continuously 
aerated using an aquarium pump at 50 mL min⁻¹. All MFC experiments were conducted at 25 ± 1 °C. 
2.3. Performance Measurements 
The cell voltage across a fixed external resistance of 1000 Ω was recorded at 10 min intervals using a Keithley 
2700 data acquisition system (Tektronix). Power density (PD, mW m⁻²) and current density were calculated as 
PD = V × I / A and J = I / A, respectively, where A is the effective membrane area (20 cm²). COD was 
measured every 24 h by UV-Vis spectrophotometry (HACH DR3900) after 1:10 dilution and closed reflux 
digestion at 150 °C for 2 h using HACH TNT plus vials. COD removal efficiency (CODr, %) was calculated 
as CODr = (COD_in - COD_out) / COD_in × 100. Coulombic efficiency (CE, %) was calculated from the 
measured current profile using CE = M ∫ I dt / (F b V_an ΔCOD), where M is the molecular weight of oxygen 
(32 g mol⁻¹), F is Faraday's constant (96,485 C mol⁻¹), b is the number of electrons transferred per mole of 
oxygen equivalent (4), and V_an is the anode volume. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was 
performed at open-circuit voltage using an Autolab PGSTAT302N potentiostat/galvanostat with a 10 mV 
sinusoidal perturbation over the frequency range from 100 kHz to 10 mHz. Internal resistance (RI) was 
obtained from equivalent-circuit fitting using a modified Randles circuit containing solution resistance (R_s), 
charge-transfer resistance (R_ct), a constant phase element, and a Warburg diffusion element. The reported RI 
is the sum of R_s and R_ct. All experiments were conducted in biological triplicate, and the results are 
reported as mean ± standard deviation. 
2.4. Membrane Characterization: SEM 
The surface and cross-sectional morphologies of the membranes were examined using scanning electron 
microscopy (SEM; JEOL JSM-7610F, Tokyo, Japan) at an accelerating voltage of 10 kV and a working 
distance of 6.4 mm. Before imaging, membrane samples were sputter-coated with a 10 nm gold layer to 
prevent charging. Cross-sectional specimens were prepared by freeze-fracturing in liquid nitrogen. Surface 
images were collected at 10,000× magnification to examine MXene distribution, pore structure, and EPS 
deposition. After 30 days of MFC operation, used membranes were carefully removed, rinsed three times with 
PBS to remove loosely attached biomass, fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde in PBS for 2 h at 4 °C, dehydrated 
through a graded ethanol series (50, 70, 90, and 100% v/v), and dried by critical point drying to preserve the 
biofilm morphology.

in increments of 0.5 pH units). A full combinatorial design across these levels would require 125 experiments.

2.6. ANN Model Development 
A feed-forward multilayer perceptron (MLP) was implemented in MATLAB R2023a using the Neural 
Network Toolbox. The network architecture was determined by systematically comparing architectures with 1-
4 hidden layers and 5-50 neurons per layer using 10-fold cross-validation. The final 3-25-20-10-5-4 
architecture, containing four hidden layers with 25, 20, 10, and 5 neurons, was selected because it produced the 
minimum validation mean squared error (MSE) without evidence of overfitting. Hyperbolic tangent sigmoid 
activation functions were used in the hidden layers, and a linear activation function was applied at the output 
layer to allow unconstrained output prediction. All input and output variables were normalized to the range [-1, 
1] using min-max normalization. The 50-point dataset was randomly divided into training (70%, n = 35), 
validation (15%, n = 7), and test (15%, n = 8) subsets. The Levenberg-Marquardt backpropagation algorithm 
was used for weight optimization because of its fast convergence for medium-sized neural networks. Training 
was terminated when the validation MSE did not decrease for six consecutive epochs. The network was trained 
20 times from random initial weights, and the run with the lowest validation MSE was retained. Table 2 
summarizes the ANN architecture and training parameters.

2.7. Multi-Objective Optimization 
Following training, the validated ANN was used as a surrogate model to generate predictions across the full 
input domain. The grid resolution was set to 0.1 wt% for MXene loading, 50 mg L⁻¹ for initial COD, and 0.05 
pH units for anode pH, producing 172,629 candidate operating conditions. For each candidate, the ANN 
predicted PD, CODr, CE, and RI. A dimensionless composite performance score was calculated by 
normalizing each output to the range [0, 1] and aggregating the objectives as Score = PD_n + CODr_n + CE_n 
+ (1 - RI_n), where the subscript n denotes normalized values and the term (1 - RI_n) represents the 
minimization of internal resistance. Equal weighting was applied to the four objectives. To test the robustness 
of the identified condition, the optimization was repeated using weights of 0.4:0.3:0.2:0.1 for 
PD:CODr:CE:RI. The same optimum was obtained within the grid resolution, confirming that the selected 
operating point was not sensitive to moderate changes in objective weighting. 
2.8. Experimental Validation 
The ANN-predicted optimum (MXene = 4.2 wt%, initial COD = 2300 mg L⁻¹, and anode pH = 7.1) was 
validated experimentally using three independent MFC assemblies. Each assembly was prepared with a freshly 
fabricated 4.2 wt% SPEEK/MXene membrane and operated for 10 days under continuous monitoring. Steady-
state performance was calculated from the average values recorded between days 7 and 10. The ANN-
predicted values were compared with the experimental values using absolute error, relative error, and overall 
prediction accuracy, defined as 100 minus the mean absolute percentage error across the four outputs.

Complementary physicochemical characterization was performed to support the SEM observations. Ion-
exchange capacity was measured by back-titration with NaOH after protonation in 1 M HCl. Water uptake was 
calculated by gravimetric comparison of wet and dry membrane masses after 24 h immersion in deionized 
water at 25 °C. The swelling ratio was determined from dimensional measurements before and after hydration. 
Proton conductivity was measured by EIS using a four-probe conductivity cell at 25 °C. Contact angle was 
determined by the sessile-drop method to evaluate surface hydrophilicity. Tensile strength and elongation at 
break were measured by uniaxial tensile testing. These characterization results were consistent with the SEM 
morphology and ANN performance trends, confirming that 4.2 wt% MXene provides the best balance of 
proton conductivity, mechanical integrity, low swelling, and hydrophilicity among the investigated 
membranes. 
Fig. 1 presents the SEM surface morphology of SPEEK/MXene membranes at MXene loadings of 0, 1, 3, 4.2, 
5, and 7 wt%, together with the cross-sectional image of the optimized 4.2 wt% membrane. The gradual 
change in surface morphology with MXene content shows a coherent trend and explains the observed 
electrochemical performance. 
At 0 wt% MXene, the bare SPEEK membrane surface is extensively covered with dense extracellular 
polymeric substance (EPS) deposits and bacterial rods, confirming severe biofouling during MFC operation. 
The absence of MXene leaves the membrane surface insufficiently hydrophilic to resist microbial adhesion 
under high-organic-load anolyte conditions. At 1 and 3 wt% MXene, the biofilm coverage decreases 
progressively, which is attributed to the increased surface hydrophilicity provided by the -OH and =O groups 
on the MXene nanosheets. This hydrophilic surface reduces bacterial attachment by creating an anti-adhesive 
hydration layer. 
At 4.2 wt% MXene, the membrane surface is uniform, with clear circular pore openings, homogeneously 
distributed MXene nanosheets, and negligible EPS or bacterial attachment. This loading represents the 
structural optimum because the nanosheets are well dispersed and do not form continuous agglomerates. Under 
this condition, the membrane maintains pore connectivity for proton transport while providing sufficient 
hydrophilic surface area for antifouling. At 5 wt%, the surface remains relatively clean but shows early platelet 
crowding near the pore edges. At 7 wt%, agglomeration becomes pronounced, pore regularity is disrupted, 
surface cracks appear, and biofouling re-emerges at defect sites [18,19]. 
The cross-sectional image of the 4.2 wt% membrane (Fig. 1g) shows the asymmetric morphology produced by 
phase inversion. The structure consists of a thin dense selective skin layer, a middle region with finger-like 
macrovoids, and a spongy microporous sublayer containing embedded MXene platelets. This morphology is 
favourable for MFC operation because the dense skin layer supports proton selectivity and substrate rejection, 
while the macrovoid and spongy sublayers reduce mass-transfer resistance. The embedded MXene platelets 
also provide localized hydrogen-bonded pathways that enhance proton migration without requiring a 
continuous conductive phase across the membrane thickness.

3.4. Effect of MXene Loading on MFC Performance 
Fig. 4 shows the effect of MXene loading on the four performance outputs at the reference condition of COD = 
2300 mg L⁻¹ and pH = 7.1. Experimental mean values are superimposed on the ANN predictions. All outputs 
show a unimodal dependence on MXene loading, with the optimum located near 4.2 wt%. This behaviour 
reflects the percolation-type effect of MXene nanosheets within the SPEEK matrix. 
Power density increased from 210 mW m⁻² at 0 wt% to a predicted maximum of 612 mW m⁻² at 4.2 wt%, and 
then decreased to 500 mW m⁻² at 7 wt%. At low and intermediate loading, well-dispersed nanosheets form 
hydrogen-bonded proton transport channels that reduce the activation energy for proton migration through the 
Grotthuss mechanism. This decreases the ohmic component of RI and improves PD. At excessive loading, 
platelet agglomeration disrupts these pathways, increases pore tortuosity, creates dead-end transport routes, 
and reduces PD [6,21]. 
CODr and CE followed the same unimodal trend, indicating that membrane composition affects both proton 
transfer and electrochemical utilization of the organic substrate. The link between these outputs is internal 
resistance: lower RI enables higher current generation, accelerates anodic glucose oxidation, and improves 
both COD consumption and coulombic conversion. The minimum RI of 63 Ω at 4.2 wt% agrees with the

effect of improved proton transport through MXene-assisted hydrogen-bonded networks and favourable 
microbial metabolism at near-neutral pH. Exoelectrogenic microorganisms, including Geobacter and 
Shewanella species, commonly show maximum current output around pH 6.8-7.5 [8,9]. At pH below 6.5, 
proton accumulation in the anode biofilm suppresses microbial activity and electron transfer. At pH above 7.5, 
competing cation transport lowers proton selectivity and reduces CE. RI shows the inverse trend and reaches 
its minimum at the same pH and MXene loading, confirming that MXene-assisted proton transport is most 
effective under near-neutral conditions [22].

candidate conditions identified MXene = 4.2 wt%, COD = 2300 mg L⁻¹, and pH = 7.1 as the global multi-
objective optimum, with a score of 3.62 out of 4.0. The second-best condition (MXene = 4.0 wt%, COD =

optimum region is robust and that minor deviations from the setpoint are unlikely to cause abrupt performance 
losses. Tables 4 and 5 present the recommended operating condition and the corresponding ANN-predicted

3.9. Comparison with Literature 
Table 8 compares the optimized performance of the present SPEEK/MXene system with selected SPEEK-
based, MXene-based, and Nafion-based MFC membranes. The 4.2 wt% SPEEK/MXene membrane produced 
an experimentally validated PD of 617.6 mW m⁻². This value is higher than the sPEEK-sZnO nanocomposite 
membrane reported by Kugarajah et al. [10] (142 mW m⁻²) and the MXene-PVDF-HFP membrane reported 
previously (approximately 260 mW m⁻²) [30]. It also exceeds the Nafion 117 system optimized using EEFO 
(478.9 mW m⁻²) [16] and the AI-optimized swarm-fuzzy Nafion system (approximately 420 mW m⁻²) [13]. 
The Nafion-based system reported by Rezk et al. [16] achieved higher COD removal (81.9%) than the present 
system (67.4%), which may be attributed to the high proton conductivity of perfluorosulfonic acid under the 
selected operating conditions. Nevertheless, the present SPEEK/MXene membrane produced higher PD at a 
substantially lower estimated material cost. SPEEK synthesis and MXene incorporation are estimated to cost 
approximately 15-20% of the equivalent Nafion 117 membrane area. The optimized SPEEK/MXene 
membrane also showed improved resistance to biofouling, which is critical for long-term MFC operation under 
high-organic-load wastewater conditions. In addition, the ANN-based framework used here is more 
comprehensive than earlier single-output fuzzy or experimental approaches because it optimizes four coupled 
performance indicators simultaneously.

4. Conclusion 
This study developed and experimentally validated an ANN-based multi-objective optimization framework for 
a SPEEK/MXene nanocomposite PEM MFC. The main conclusions are summarized as follows. 
The ANN model trained on 50 experimental observations achieved an overall R² of 0.96 for the four outputs, 
namely PD, CODr, CE, and RI. The prediction accuracy exceeded 93.8% for each individual response. The 
model successfully captured the nonlinear interactions among MXene loading, initial COD, and anode pH and 
generated a response surface that was experimentally verifiable and physically interpretable. 
SEM characterization confirmed that 4.2 wt% MXene produced a uniformly dispersed nanosheet network, 
clear membrane pores, and negligible EPS accumulation after 30 days of MFC operation. In contrast, the 
membrane without MXene showed severe EPS deposition and bacterial adhesion because of insufficient 
surface hydrophilicity, while 7 wt% MXene caused nanosheet agglomeration, surface defects, and renewed 
biofouling. 
Sensitivity analysis identified the main operating hierarchy of the system. MXene loading mainly controlled 
PD and RI, initial COD mainly controlled CODr, and anode pH mainly controlled CE through its effect on 
microbial activity and proton availability at the biofilm-electrode interface. These findings indicate which 
operating variables should be prioritized during practical MFC design and control. 
The ANN-predicted optimum at MXene = 4.2 wt%, COD = 2300 mg L⁻¹, and pH = 7.1 was confirmed 
experimentally in triplicate. The validated outputs were PD = 617.6 mW m⁻², CODr = 67.4%, CE = 17.2%, 
and RI = 64.5 Ω. The corresponding prediction errors were 0.9%, 2.1%, 6.2%, and 2.3%, respectively, and the 
overall prediction accuracy was 97.1%. The near-zero-centred error distribution further confirmed the absence 
of systematic model bias. 
The three-dimensional response surfaces showed that high PD and CODr are obtained near neutral pH (6.8-
7.3) and intermediate COD concentrations (2000-2500 mg L⁻¹). These ranges define practical operating 
windows that can tolerate moderate variation in wastewater substrate composition. 
Compared with selected literature benchmarks, the optimized 4.2 wt% SPEEK/MXene membrane achieved a 
PD of 617.6 mW m⁻², exceeding Nafion 117-based systems optimized using meta-heuristic algorithms (478.9 
and approximately 420 mW m⁻²). The membrane also offers lower estimated material cost and improved 
antifouling resistance. The proposed ANN framework is more comprehensive than single-output optimization 
methods because it evaluates four key performance indicators simultaneously. 
The obtained results demonstrate that the ANN-based optimization method can be transferred to other 
nanocomposite membrane systems in which filler loading, substrate conditions, and electrolyte chemistry
